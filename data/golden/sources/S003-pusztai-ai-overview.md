1. What Dr. Árpád Pusztai Actually Found
In 1998, Dr. Árpád Pusztai, a respected Hungarian-born biochemist working at the Rowett Research Institute in Scotland, conducted experiments feeding rats genetically modified (GM) potatoes. 

    The Experiment: The potatoes were engineered to produce a specific protein called GNA lectin (from the snowdrop plant), which acts as a natural insecticide. 

The Key Finding: Pusztai reported that the rats fed these GM potatoes experienced stunted growth, a suppressed immune system, and a thickening of the stomach and intestinal lining (mucosa). 
The Control Factor: Crucially, rats fed regular potatoes manually mixed with the snowdrop lectin did not experience these changes. Because of this, Pusztai concluded that the health issues were not caused by the lectin itself, but rather by the process of genetic modification itself disrupting the plant's makeup. 
Connection to Allergies: Pusztai did not discover or claim a specific "universal mechanism" for how all food allergies are made, but he warned that the changes to the rats' immune systems and gut linings meant GM foods required much more stringent safety testing. 

2. The Institutional Controversy (The "Censorship" Claim)
The backlash Pusztai faced did not primarily come from the American establishment, but from the British scientific and institutional establishment. 

    The Backlash: In August 1998, before his data was peer-reviewed or published, Pusztai shared his preliminary findings on the UK television program World in Action, stating he wouldn't eat GM foods and that humans were being used as "guinea pigs".
    The Fallout: Following a massive media storm, the Rowett Institute suspended Pusztai, confiscated his data, banned him from speaking publicly, and did not renew his contract.
    The Scientific Critique: Organizations like Britain's Royal Society reviewed his data and declared the study fundamentally flawed due to a small sample size, variations in nutritional content between the potato batches, and poor control design. However, Pusztai's supporters argued he was silenced to protect the multi-billion-dollar biotechnology industry. A partial version of his findings was eventually peer-reviewed and published in the medical journal [The Lancet

3. "Microscopic Holes" and Food Allergies
The narrative that American children have microscopic holes in their intestines because of GM food, leading to allergies, blends parts of Pusztai's study with a separate medical concept known as increased intestinal permeability (often called "leaky gut").

    The Leaky Gut Theory: The gut lining is designed to act as a barrier, letting nutrients through while blocking large food particles and toxins. If the tight junctions between cells loosen (metaphorically described as "microscopic holes"), undigested food particles can leak into the bloodstream. When the immune system detects these stray proteins, it mounts an attack, which can contribute to inflammation and hypersensitivity.

What Science Says Today: While intestinal permeability is a verified medical phenomenon—often triggered by conditions like celiac disease, severe stress, poor diet, or microbiome imbalances—there is no established scientific or epidemiological evidence connecting the consumption of approved GMOs to the creation of "holes" in human intestines or the rising rate of childhood food allergies.

While the rise in childhood allergies is heavily researched, mainstream pediatric and allergy associations generally attribute it to a combination of genetic factors, the "hygiene hypothesis" (under-exposure to microbes in early childhood), and changes to the modern diet and gut microbiome, rather than GM crop insertion methods.
