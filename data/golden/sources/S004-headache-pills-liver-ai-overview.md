Headache pills—particularly those containing acetaminophen
(also known as paracetamol or Tylenol) or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) like ibuprofen—can cause liver damage, usually when taken in excess, combined with alcohol, or used long-term. 
Acetaminophen (Paracetamol)

    How it affects the liver: The liver breaks down acetaminophen into a byproduct called NAPQI. In normal doses, your liver neutralizes this byproduct easily. In large amounts, NAPQI builds up and causes oxidative stress, leading to sudden (acute) liver injury or failure. 

Dosing limits: For most healthy adults, the maximum daily limit is 4,000 mg per day, though many doctors recommend staying under 3,000 mg or 2,000 mg if you use it frequently, have chronic liver disease, or regularly drink alcohol. 
The hidden risk: Acetaminophen is an active ingredient in more than 600 over-the-counter and prescription drugs, including cold, flu, and sinus medications. Taking a headache pill alongside a multi-symptom cold medicine can accidentally push you over the safe limit. 
Alcohol interaction: Drinking alcohol causes your liver to convert more acetaminophen into toxic byproducts, meaning lower doses can become harmful. 

NSAIDs (Ibuprofen, Naproxen, Aspirin)

    NSAIDs are processed differently and are generally less toxic to the liver than acetaminophen, though heavy, chronic, or daily use can still strain or damage the liver, especially in people who already have liver disease or drink alcohol heavily. They can also affect the stomach and kidneys. 

Warning Signs of Liver Trouble
If you suspect an overdose or experience severe liver strain, signs include:

    Nausea or vomiting
    Upper abdominal or stomach pain (especially on the right side)
    Jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes)
    Extreme fatigue or confusion 

(If you or someone else has taken too much acetaminophen, seek emergency medical care or contact your local poison control center
immediately.) You can review safety tips on the U.S. Food and Drug Administration Consumer Updates
or consult the Mayo Clinic Guide on Liver Disease and Medications
.
